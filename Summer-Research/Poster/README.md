# Muscle Coactivation Levels Reflect a Tradeoff Between Metabolic Cost and Performance When Responding to Physical Disturbances During Upper Limb Posture Control

## Summary

Muscle coactivation — the simultaneous activation of agonist and antagonist muscles — improves motor
performance by letting muscles generate force more quickly and respond more effectively to sensory
feedback, but at a metabolic cost that had not previously been characterized for upper-limb posture
control. This poster presents early results from the Cluff Lab investigating that tradeoff.

**Hypothesis:** Higher levels of muscle coactivation improve performance (faster movement, lower error)
at the expense of increased metabolic cost.

**Methods:** Five healthy participants held a fixed arm posture in a Kinarm exoskeleton robot while
receiving mechanical disturbances. Biofeedback controlled coactivation at 0.5x, 1x, 2x, 4x, 8x, and 16x
each participant's self-selected level, with metabolic cost measured via gas respirometry and muscle
activity via surface EMG. Testing continued until failure.

**Key results:**
- Higher coactivation reduced peak displacement and movement time after a perturbation.
- Higher coactivation increased responsiveness to proprioceptive feedback (larger stretch responses in
  the agonist, greater inhibition of the antagonist).
- Higher coactivation reduced error rate but increased metabolic cost.

**Conclusion:** There is a complex interplay between metabolic cost and performance in the control of
human arm movements and posture — at spontaneous coactivation levels, participants prioritize minimizing
metabolic cost over performance, while biofeedback-driven increases in coactivation trade metabolic cost
for improved performance.
