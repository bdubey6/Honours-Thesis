# Day et al. (1991) — Changes in the response to magnetic and electrical stimulation of the motor cortex following muscle stretch in man

**Journal:** Journal of Physiology, 433, 41–57

## Introduction
- **Hypothesis:** An increase in motor cortical activity following muscle stretch is accompanied by a
  change in the excitability of the motor cortex to a second (test) stimulus.
- Sub-questions: (1) Does muscle stretch increase motor cortex excitability? (2) Do magnetic vs. electrical
  cortical stimulation differ in their sensitivity to this effect?

## Methods
- 8 subjects (of an initial 12; 4 excluded for technical failures/pilot conditions).
- Forearm semi-pronated, fingers on levers connected to torque motors; muscle stretch induced by increasing
  motor torque (0.5 → 1.5 N·m over 1 s); EMG recorded from flexor digitorum profundus (FDP).
- Cortex stimulated magnetically (and, in a separate experiment, electrically) at varying intensities and
  intervals relative to the stretch.
- Independent variables: interval between stretch and cortical stimulus, stimulus intensity, stimulation
  type (magnetic vs. electrical). Dependent variables: EMG response amplitude, stretch reflex component
  latencies.
- Open question at the time: why lignocaine (local anesthetic) injection was used (to block afferent input
  from cutaneous/joint/muscle receptors at the wrist and isolate the contribution of muscle receptors).

## Results
- EMG response to magnetic stimulation was larger when timed to coincide with the long-latency stretch
  reflex (LLSR) than with the short-latency stretch reflex (SLSR) or background tonic activity.
- This facilitation occurred even when the magnetic stimulus was below the threshold to produce a response
  on its own.
- The time course of facilitation matched the time course of the LLSR.
- The facilitatory effect persisted after blocking cutaneous/joint/muscle afferents at the wrist —
  implicating muscle receptor input specifically.

## Discussion
- Interpretation: the short-latency stretch reflex is mediated purely by spinal circuitry, while the
  long-latency component has a transcortical contribution — muscle stretch transiently raises motor cortex
  excitability, and this raised excitability specifically coincides with the LLSR window.
- Electrical stimulation did not show the same facilitation, likely because it activates corticospinal
  neurons differently (more directly) than magnetic stimulation.
