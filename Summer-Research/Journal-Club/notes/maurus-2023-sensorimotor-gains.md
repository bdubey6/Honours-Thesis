# Maurus et al. (2023) — The nervous system tunes sensorimotor gains when reaching in variable mechanical environments

**Journal:** iScience, 26(6), 106756
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.isci.2023.106756](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2023.106756)

## Introduction
- **Research question:** How does the nervous system change its control of reaching when facing
  unpredictable, time-varying physical disturbances?
- **Hypothesis:** The nervous system becomes more responsive to sensory feedback (i.e., increases feedback
  gains) under these conditions.
- **Motivation:** Everyday tasks (e.g., drinking water on a bumpy train) can be disturbed unpredictably;
  understanding how control adapts to this is the goal.

## Methods
- Healthy participants performed reaching tasks in a Kinarm robotic exoskeleton.
- Random, time-varying torque disturbances were applied during reaching.
- Hand paths, velocities, and muscle responses (EMG) were measured, including with mechanical probes
  (constant step-torque perturbations) and visual probes (cursor jumps) used to assay feedback gains.

## Results
- Participants adjusted their control strategy: faster and more robust movements, with peak forward
  velocity increasing and shifting earlier in the movement.
- Increased responsiveness (feedback gains) to both proprioceptive and visual feedback.
- Hand paths became more variable during exposure to disturbances, but participants still reached the
  target accurately.
- When probed with the same mechanical perturbation, hand displacement was smaller during the exposure
  phase than baseline/washout — i.e., better corrective responses.
- **Experiment 2** (low vs. high disturbance variability): feedback gains scaled with the variability of
  the disturbance — higher variability led to even larger/faster corrective responses.

## Why the visual-feedback experiment matters
- If the underlying control *policy* has truly changed (not just muscle stiffness/co-contraction), then
  responsiveness to *visual* feedback should increase too — visual feedback isn't affected by muscle
  stiffness the way proprioceptive/mechanical responses could be. This helps rule out "it's just stiffness"
  as the sole explanation and supports a genuine change in feedback gain/control policy (robust optimal
  feedback control).

## Notes
- This connects directly to the Burdet/Franklin impedance-control work: both are about how the CNS
  modulates its response to instability/disturbance, but Maurus emphasizes *feedback gain* / control-policy
  changes (including to visual feedback) rather than only mechanical impedance (stiffness/co-contraction).
